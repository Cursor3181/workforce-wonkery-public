Data decoded

California is hiring, cutting, and changing at the same time.

On the San Jose–Campbell border, patients are walking into a new 10-story public health center with more than 200 exam rooms. In Marina, Joby Aviation says it is adding shifts as it scales aircraft production. In the Bay Area, tech companies disclosed plans for more than 1,100 job cuts in September. Those are not three different economies. They are three views of California's labor market.

That is why August's +39,400 jobs is a good headline and an incomplete story.

Updated monthly after California’s official employment release

Scene 1 · Where the growth is

Health care is doing much of the pulling.

California's biggest August gain came from private education and health services: 18,600 jobs. Over the past year, that sector added 131,500, far more than any other major industry.

You can see the demand taking physical shape in San Jose. The new Valley Health Center San Jose-Campbell brings more than a dozen services into a 230,000-square-foot facility with more than 200 exam rooms. It does not mean every position there is new, but it is a vivid example of where California is building capacity.

Schools reopening helped government add another 9,800 jobs. Leisure and hospitality and trade also grew. Meanwhile, professional and business services, information, finance, construction, and several other sectors moved the other way.

Scene 2 · The road is still bumpy

One good month does not make a boom.

August reversed July's loss, but the last year looks less like a clean climb and more like stop-and-go traffic. California gained payroll jobs in 8 of the past 13 months. January surged by 81,800. February fell. March jumped. July fell again. Then came August.

That unevenness shows up inside industries too. Statewide manufacturing added just 800 jobs in August and remained 10,100 jobs below its level a year earlier. Yet in Marina, Joby said in May that it was adding a third shift to parts of its production operation and that composites output was running at more than 2.5 times the prior year's volume.

For a workforce board, that distinction is crucial. A weak statewide sector can still contain a very real local training opportunity.

Scene 3 · Change the camera

A 5.1% unemployment rate can hide a lot of churn.

The employer survey counted 39,400 more payroll jobs in August. A separate household survey counted 36,600 fewer employed Californians. At the same time, the labor force shrank by 50,500 and the number of unemployed people fell by 14,000.

That is how the unemployment rate can look better while fewer residents are working. The two surveys measure different things, but together they tell a more human story: employers can be adding positions while some workers are still losing jobs, leaving the labor force, or struggling to move into the parts of the economy that are growing.

Look at the Bay Area. The region added 7,800 jobs in August, yet tech employment fell by 2,900. In September, tech companies disclosed plans to eliminate another 1,196 Bay Area jobs. A labor market can expand and still feel like a downturn if you happen to work in the wrong corner of it.

Scene 4 · The paycheck

Average pay is rising. That still does not tell us who is winning.

Private-sector hourly earnings reached $42.54 in August, up 3.1% from a year earlier. That is a meaningful positive signal.

But an average can smooth over the same differences the job counts reveal. A health care worker entering a growing field, a software worker facing a layoff, and a manufacturing technician joining an expanding production line can all live inside the same statewide wage number.

The useful question is not simply whether the average is rising. It is whether more workers can reach jobs that pay enough, last long enough, and offer a path forward.

California is not stalled. It is not booming. It is reshuffling where the opportunities are.

For workforce leaders, the headline question is not “Did California add jobs?” It is where is demand durable enough to build around, and who risks being left behind as the mix changes?
