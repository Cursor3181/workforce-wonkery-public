# Workforce Wonkery Practice Feature Editorial Standard

**Status:** Canonical editorial standard  
**Applies to:** flagship promising-practice briefs, national/state practice features, operating-model explainers, and other long-form Practice pieces that synthesize multiple examples.  
**Canonical exemplar:** [Before the WARN: What Real Layoff Aversion Looks Like](https://workforcewonkery.com/2026/10/05/before-the-warn-real-layoff-aversion/)

## Purpose

A Workforce Wonkery Practice feature should not read like a catalog of programs or a research memo.

Its job is to help a California workforce reader understand a problem, see how real systems have responded, weigh the evidence, and leave with a clearer decision model.

The standard is:

**Story first. Evidence inside the story. California implication as the payoff.**

## Default narrative architecture

Use this structure unless the subject clearly requires another form.

1. **Open with a real operating moment**
   - Begin with a documented employer, worker, program, board, or system facing a concrete problem.
   - Establish stakes quickly.
   - Do not invent a person, quote, motive, or detail that is not documented.
   - A useful opening creates a decision question, not merely a topic introduction.

2. **State the question the system had to answer**
   - Example pattern: "What should the workforce system do?"
   - Make the reader understand why the normal or obvious response may be inadequate.

3. **Use the opening case as the narrative spine**
   - Return to it when useful.
   - Do not abandon the opening after one paragraph and switch into a state-by-state inventory.

4. **Organize examples around decisions or problems, not jurisdictions**
   - Prefer headings such as:
     - What if the business itself is in trouble?
     - What if the downturn is temporary?
     - What if the owner is leaving?
     - What if the closure cannot be stopped?
   - Avoid a sequence that is only "Pennsylvania / Michigan / California / New Jersey" unless geography is itself the analytical point.

5. **Make quantitative evidence advance the story**
   - Put the number where it answers a reader question.
   - Explain why the number matters.
   - Pair scale, cost, outcome, or trend data with the operating story that produced it.
   - Do not add unrelated metrics because they are available.
   - Do not total "jobs saved" or similar metrics across programs unless definitions, periods, and methods are truly comparable.

6. **Translate system numbers back to people**
   - Show what a program outcome means for workers, employers, or communities when the source supports that interpretation.
   - Do not invent individual stories.
   - Distinguish documented outcomes from reasonable implications.

7. **Include the uncomfortable measurement question**
   - Ask how the claimed result was verified.
   - Separate activity from outcome.
   - Distinguish program-reported outcomes from independently evaluated or follow-up-verified evidence.
   - Flag older but still instructive evidence as historical.

8. **Synthesize before recommending**
   - After the stories, give the reader a compact decision model, table, ladder, or diagnostic.
   - The synthesis should answer: "What problem am I actually trying to solve?"

9. **Make California the climax, not an appendix**
   - Show what California already has.
   - Identify the missing connection, operating choice, or capacity.
   - Translate national practice without implying that authority, funding, or results automatically transfer.

10. **End with a callback**
    - Return to the opening case, question, or tension.
    - Finish with one memorable conclusion that captures the operating lesson.
    - The ending should feel earned by the evidence, not like a slogan added afterward.

## Evidence + story rules

- Every concrete factual claim must be supported by the governed evidence record or an authoritative source.
- Prefer primary sources, official reports, evaluation documents, board records, and current program materials.
- Use qualitative detail to explain how the practice operated.
- Use quantitative data to show scale, cost, outcomes, trend, or contrast.
- Never manufacture a worker quote, employer quote, internal motivation, causal claim, or outcome.
- If the public record cannot establish a direct worker outcome, say what the evidence does and does not show.
- Distinguish:
  - **documented fact**
  - **program-reported outcome**
  - **independent evaluation**
  - **Workforce Wonkery interpretation**
  - **California translation**
- Historical examples may be used when the operating model remains useful, but current status and rules must be checked before recommending replication.

## Voice

The voice should be:

- conversational but evidence-led;
- curious rather than bureaucratic;
- clear enough for a busy WDB director, board member, fiscal lead, or program manager;
- willing to ask the harder question behind the metric;
- practical without becoming a checklist too early;
- skeptical of easy claims without becoming cynical.

Avoid:

- research-memo sequencing;
- long lists of programs before the reader understands the problem;
- blocks of statistics with no interpretation;
- generic "best practice" language;
- promotional claims repeated as fact;
- assuming that more content equals more value.

## Quantitative integration test

Before publication, inspect every major number and ask:

1. What reader question does this number answer?
2. Is the denominator, period, geography, or method clear enough?
3. Is it comparable to nearby numbers?
4. Is it program-reported or independently verified?
5. Would the paragraph be stronger without it?

If a number does not help the reader understand the decision, remove or relocate it.

## Feature synthesis device

Long-form Practice features should normally include one compact synthesis device after the evidence, such as:

- signal -> likely problem -> tool -> example;
- problem -> intervention -> evidence -> California adaptation;
- outcome ladder;
- operating sequence;
- decision tree.

The synthesis should follow the stories, not replace them.

## California translation test

A strong California section answers:

- Which parts already exist here?
- What is missing?
- Who would own the missing function?
- What can a Local WDB do directly?
- What requires EDD, another agency, an intermediary, or another funding source?
- What would need to be verified before replication?

## Ending test

The final paragraphs should make the reader remember the operating lesson rather than the list of examples.

A good ending:
- returns to the opening;
- narrows the conclusion;
- states what changes when the system acts differently;
- leaves the reader with a practical mental model.

## Editorial QA

For a flagship Practice feature, the evaluator must be able to answer **yes** to all of these:

- Is there a documented narrative spine?
- Does the opening create stakes within the first few paragraphs?
- Are examples organized around reader decisions rather than geography?
- Are quantitative and qualitative evidence integrated rather than separated?
- Does at least one section translate system outcomes back to workers or employers?
- Does the piece address evidence quality and measurement limits?
- Is there a compact synthesis device?
- Does California appear as the analytical payoff rather than a final add-on?
- Does the ending call back to the opening?
- Can a California WDB reader explain what they would do differently after reading it?

If not, revise before publication.
